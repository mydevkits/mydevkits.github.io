Tooling

Two applications, and the failures they remove

Both are built and working. Each one addresses a recurring failure that training alone does not prevent, because both happen at the moment somebody is busy and under pressure to move fast.

Application one

Portability check, for sales

The failure it removes. A number is promised during a sale. The deal closes. Then we discover the number cannot move to us. At that point there are no good options left: the customer has signed on the strength of keeping a number that is on their signage, their website, their referral pads and in their patients' phones. In the worst version they have already canceled their old service, at which point the number cannot even be forwarded, because the account that held it is gone.

I have watched this happen many times from the sales side. I came up through account management, so I have seen what it costs a practice when a number they were promised turns out not to be movable, and I have seen how reliably it happens even to conscientious people working at pace. That is why I treat it as a tooling problem rather than a discipline problem.

A rep who has to email support and wait a day is likely to guess. A rep who can check in thirty seconds is likely to check. Speed is most of the design problem.

What it does. One field. The rep types the number and gets an answer they can act on immediately: it can move, it cannot, or this one needs us to look at it. No jargon, no telecoms vocabulary, nothing that needs explaining. A negative result routes to an exception path with an answer inside one business day, because a gate with no way through it gets worked around.

How it should be deployed. Inside the tool the reps already use, and as a required result on the opportunity before it can be marked closed won. That last part matters more than the tool itself: an optional field is not a gate, and under quota pressure good people skip optional fields.

What it is worth. Every port rejection is categorizable and most are preventable. Rejection reasons by category is one of the numbers I will report monthly, and a portability check at the front of the process is the single largest lever on it. It also converts a category of angry post sale escalation into a conversation that happens while the deal is still open, when it is still a sales problem with sales options.

Application two

Account record reader

The failure it removes. To move a number, the losing carrier has to accept our request, and they check it against their own records with no tolerance for approximation. The account number has to match. The main billing number on the account has to match, and it is frequently not the number being moved. The business name has to match character for character, including the part that looks like a typo. The service address has to match, including the suite formatting.

Those details get read off a bill or a service record by a person, under time pressure, and typed into a port order. Every one of them is a chance to introduce a mismatch, and a mismatch is a rejection: the date is gone, the customer has to be told, and the whole cycle restarts.

The trap after a rejection is worse than the rejection. People resubmit the same paperwork that just failed, which is the most common cause of a second rejection immediately following the first.

What it does. Reads the carrier service record and extracts the ten fields a port order actually needs, each with a copy button, so nothing is retyped. It removes transcription error as a category rather than reducing it.

What it is worth. Transcription accounts for a meaningful share of the preventable rejections, and unlike the others it cannot be trained away, because it is a data entry failure rather than a knowledge failure. It also compresses the collection step, which is one of the slowest parts of time to activation.

Why these two

Both sit before the failure, not after it

Support tooling usually gets built to help people recover from problems faster. These two are deliberately the other kind. They sit at the two points where an error is cheap to prevent and expensive to fix afterwards: the moment a number is promised, and the moment its details are transcribed.

Neither replaces judgement, and neither is a substitute for the procedures. What they do is make the correct action faster than the incorrect one, which is the only reliable way to change what busy people do.

The measure I would hold both to is rejection reasons by category, month over month. If the categories these two address do not shrink, the tools are not working and I would say so.

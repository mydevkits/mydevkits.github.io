Ninety day plan

Thirteen weeks, by week

Three phases. Learn and change nothing for four weeks, build for five, prove it for four.

Phases

Why it is ordered this way

The first four weeks are deliberately not productive in an obvious sense. Arriving with changes in week one is the most common way a new lead gets a team quietly working around them, and the current state cannot be improved before it is understood. The building phase then runs in order of what each missing procedure costs when it is absent, rather than in the order the work is easiest.

Week 1
Get operational, and start the questions log. Access, orientation, one to ones with each person, and a log of every question I have to ask somebody. That log is the first draft of the manual and the ability to write it disappears after about a fortnight.
Week 2
Learn the work as it actually happens. Every open port order read as a list. A cutover watched, or the last one reconstructed hour by hour. Every rejection in the last ninety days categorized and counted. Emergency address position sampled.
Week 3
Map the current state and find the seams. The end to end path drawn from what I watched rather than what people describe. Onboarding, sales and application support interviewed on where work falls between teams.
Week 4
Measure, then write the first procedure. Metric set chosen and defined. Instrumentation on whatever the ticketing system gives free. The procedure that prevents the most expensive failure written, then tested on the newest person. Thirty day review.
Week 5
The procedures that prevent disasters. Three of them, in order of what they cost when missing: do not cancel early, verify portability before promising, register the emergency address on every handset.
Week 6
The porting playbook, end to end. Every path a number can take and every way each one fails, written one step per screen with the forms attached. Consolidated and corrected by the team.
Week 7
The sales portability gate. The highest value change available, and the hardest, because it changes another team's process. Designed with sales rather than imposed on them, with a documented exception path so nobody has to route around it.
Week 8
The team: training and quality. Program handed to the trainer to own. Owned areas assigned with names on procedures. Quality criteria published before any review begins. Coverage agreed with workforce operations.
Week 9
Hardware and logistics. Specification by room, the four separate dates in an order, provisioning including the stale redirect claim that ruins install days, and the replacement path. Mid point review.
Week 10
Emergency calling and compliance. Audit across every live practice, not a sample. Anything missing treated as an incident and fixed the same day. Then built into the activation path as a gate.
Week 11
The vendor relationships. DialStack and Bandwidth turned from support addresses into named contacts with agreed escalation paths, known support hours and a recurring call.
Week 12
Reporting and the baseline. A quarter of numbers with reasons attached, reported as trend and cause rather than as a dashboard. Then the test: if I were out for two weeks, what stops?
Week 13
Review, and the next quarter. What changed, what did not, and what I would do differently. Three priorities for the following quarter, each tied to a number so it can be judged the same way.

Reporting

What goes to whom, and how often

Weekly, internal
Open port orders reviewed as a list. A stuck port nobody is chasing is the most common quiet failure in this function.
Monthly, Director of Onboarding
Time to activation and health check completion, with the reason behind any movement.
Monthly, VP of Support
Ticket volume, resolution time and satisfaction, paired with repeat contact rate.
Monthly, both
Port success on first submission, and rejection reasons by category. The number that says whether the function is working.
Per incident
Post incident review within the week, with at least one procedure changed as a result.
